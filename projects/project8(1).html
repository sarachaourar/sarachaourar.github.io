1. Why it matters

Around 18% of the roughly two million tonnes of oil that enters the sea each year comes from rigs and tankers — sudden, concentrated, and devastating to the coastline it hits.

Crude oil carries polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons that persist in tissue and water long after a spill, and volatile compounds that turn toxic the moment they evaporate. Slicks coat feathers and fur, strip their insulation, and drown or suffocate the animals that can't escape them.

The outcome of a spill response is largely decided in its first hours. Responders need to know where the oil is, how large it is, and which way it's moving — before the window to contain it closes. That urgency is the whole reason this project exists: can a simple, transparent algorithm deliver a usable map fast enough to matter?

2. Why radar sees what the eye can't

Synthetic Aperture Radar images through cloud and darkness — and oil happens to leave a signature it can't hide from.

The physics in one line

Wind roughens open water into small waves that scatter radar pulses back to the sensor as a bright signal. Oil dampens those same waves, smooths the surface, and sends less energy back — so a slick shows up as a dark scar on an otherwise bright sea.

The catch: look-alikes

Low wind, rain cells, upwelling, and natural surface films dampen the sea in exactly the same way. Anything that reduces backscatter can be mistaken for oil — which is the central problem this project has to reckon with, not just detect around.

3. The method: adaptive local thresholding

A single fixed threshold doesn't work — ocean brightness drifts across a scene with wind, sea state, and viewing geometry. So the threshold has to move with it.

For every pixel, the algorithm looks at its surrounding 300×300 px neighbourhood, computes the local mean backscatter, and subtracts a fixed 3.5 dB offset. Anything darker than that local, moving reference is flagged as a candidate oil pixel. 4. From API call to binary mask

A genuine sequence, run start to finish in under fifteen minutes:

  1. Query Sentinel-1 RTC via STAC — IW swath, VV polarization, fetched directly from the Microsoft Planetary Computer's open catalog — no manual download.
  2. Reproject and crop — the scene is reprojected to EPSG:4326 and cropped to the bounding box around the reported spill site.
  3. Convert to decibel scale — the VV band is transformed logarithmically (10·log₁₀) so brightness differences match how backscatter is conventionally read.
  4. Compute the local threshold surface — a 300×300 px moving window sweeps the scene, offset by 3.5 dB below each neighbourhood's mean.
  5. Generate the binary mask — pixels darker than their local threshold are flagged as oil candidates, ready for a human analyst to review.

5. Case study — Al Khiran, Kuwait, 2017

10 Aug 2017 · 02:47 UTC

On August 10, 2017, roughly 132,500 litres of crude leaked off Kuwait's Al Khiran coast, next to the Al Khafji offshore field — a real, documented incident used here to pressure-test the pipeline against ground truth.

A Sentinel-1A scene acquired the same morning was run through the full workflow and benchmarked against an independent reference delineation from SkyTruth, the environmental monitoring organization that mapped the spill by hand.

6. Where it breaks — and why that's the point

A method that only ever gets praised hasn't been tested hard enough. Three failure modes showed up clearly in the Kuwait run:

SAR speckle False positives

Instead of one coherent slick shape, the mask came back as a scatter of isolated single-pixel hits across open water — individual speckle grains dipping just far enough below the local mean to get flagged, with no real film present.

Coastal terrain look-alikes False positives

Land can return backscatter as low as an oil-covered sea surface. With no land mask applied, the Al Khiran coastline itself was flagged solidly — the same look-alike problem documented for ocean phenomena, just from a different source.

Unanalyzed border Missed detections

The 300×300 px window can't be computed within about 150 px of any image edge, leaving a NaN margin around the whole scene. Any spill sitting in that border would be silently excluded — a limitation that scales directly with window size.

7. What this project actually taught me

CLOUD-NATIVE GEOSPATIAL

Working with STAC & the Planetary Computer

Querying and streaming petabyte-scale satellite archives directly, instead of downloading full scenes — the difference between a workflow that scales and one that doesn't.

SAR IMAGE PHYSICS

Radiometric correction & backscatter

Understanding why terrain-corrected products are non-negotiable for cross-scene comparison, and how the same physical process (wave damping) both explains and confounds detection.

ALGORITHM DESIGN

Adaptive vs. global thresholding

Designing a spatially varying decision rule from first principles, and reasoning about how a moving-window parameter trades off sensitivity, noise, and edge coverage.

CRITICAL EVALUATION

Validating against independent ground truth

Benchmarking automated output against SkyTruth's manual delineation — and being precise about where the method agrees, and where it simply can't discriminate.
