Synthetic Aperture Radar: Detection of Oil Slicks via Adaptive Local Thresholding

Detection of a marine oil slick in Sentinel-1 SAR imagery using an adaptive local thresholding algorithm, validated against the August 2017 oil spill at Al Khiran, Kuwait.

Detection Result

Detection mask (top), VV backscatter in decibels (centre), and SkyTruth's manually mapped reference delineation (bottom), Al Khiran, Kuwait, 10 August 2017

1. Introduction

Oil spills are among the most damaging forms of marine pollution. Roughly 18% of the oil entering the ocean each year originates from rigs and tankers, producing sudden, concentrated slicks that can devastate the coastline they reach.

Crude oil carries polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons that persist in tissue and water long after a spill, along with volatile compounds that turn toxic on evaporation. Slicks coat feathers and fur, strip their insulating properties, and can drown or suffocate wildlife unable to escape them.

Because the outcome of a spill response is largely decided within its first hours, responders need a fast, reliable way to locate and delineate a slick. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is well suited to this task, imaging the ocean surface independent of cloud cover, daylight, or weather. Wind roughens open water into small waves that scatter radar pulses back to the sensor as a bright return; oil dampens those same waves and smooths the surface, so a slick appears as a dark patch against an otherwise bright sea. The same effect, however, is produced by low-wind areas, rain cells, upwelling, and natural surface films, none of which are oil — these look-alikes are the central problem this project has to reckon with.

This project implements a simple, transparent adaptive-thresholding algorithm for detecting oil slicks in Sentinel-1 SAR imagery, and benchmarks it against a documented spill.

2. Study Area and Event

On 10 August 2017, an estimated 132,500 litres of crude oil leaked into the sea off Al Khiran, Kuwait, near the Al Khafji offshore field.

The spill is documented and was independently mapped by SkyTruth, an environmental monitoring organisation, which provides a ground-truth reference against which the algorithm's output can be benchmarked.

The site is particularly relevant due to the availability of both satellite imagery and an independent manual delineation for the same event.

4. Methodology

The pipeline queries and processes Sentinel-1 imagery directly from a cloud-native archive, avoiding manual downloads:

  • Query Sentinel-1 RTC scene via STAC from the Microsoft Planetary Computer (IW mode, VV polarization)
  • Reproject to EPSG:4326 and crop to the bounding box of the reported spill site
  • Convert the VV band to decibel scale (10·log₁₀)
  • Compute a local mean backscatter surface using a moving 300×300 px window
  • Subtract a fixed 3.5 dB offset from the local mean to define a spatially adaptive threshold
  • Flag pixels darker than their local threshold as candidate oil pixels, producing a binary mask

The full sequence runs end-to-end in under fifteen minutes.

5. Results and Discussion

The resulting binary mask was compared against SkyTruth's independent manual delineation of the same spill. The adaptive approach successfully flagged much of the darkened area corresponding to the documented slick, showing that a simple, locally adaptive rule can produce a usable first-pass detection map.

Three limitations were evident in this run. SAR speckle produced isolated single-pixel false positives scattered across open water, where individual noise grains dipped just far enough below the local mean to be flagged despite no oil being present. The Al Khiran coastline itself was also flagged, since bare terrain can return backscatter as low as an oil-covered sea surface and no land mask was applied. Finally, the 300×300 px window cannot be computed within roughly 150 px of any image edge, leaving an unanalysed border around the scene; a spill located there would go undetected, a limitation that scales directly with window size.

Annex

a. Adaptive Threshold

T(x, y) = μ_w(x, y) − Δ

Where μ_w(x, y) is the local mean backscatter, in dB, computed over a w × w moving window centred at pixel (x, y), and Δ is a fixed offset (3.5 dB in this study). Pixels with backscatter below T(x, y) are flagged as candidate oil pixels.
